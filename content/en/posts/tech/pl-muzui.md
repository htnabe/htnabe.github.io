Since taking on the Project Lead (PL) role, I've come to understand a few things — so I'm writing them down. I want to think about ways to approach it that go beyond just "grin and bear it until the project is over."

## Introduction

Consider the following **fictional** contract project. Emphasis on **fictional**. Any resemblance to real projects you may have seen is purely coincidental.

- Project scale: 6 months to 1 year
- Team composition
  - One experienced senior employee acting as PM
  - A small number of full-time engineers plus several partner staff from affiliated companies
    - Partner staff skill levels vary widely
  - I'm leading a team like this
    - Of course, I'm also writing code and doing design work
- Client staff range from technically literate to completely non-technical. Basically random.

## My Background

A few months of PL experience. I understand frontend technology reasonably well, and I can implement even unfamiliar technologies by researching best practices. Most of my past work has been solo projects, but over the last six months my opportunities to work in teams have skyrocketed.

I've never read a book on project leadership or project management — I didn't expect to be put in this position so soon.

Ideally, I should read at least one well-regarded book on the subject. If you know a good one, please let me know.

## Reflections on Being a Project Lead

In a word: I'm not enjoying it. The single biggest reason is the inability to accumulate success experiences continuously. Or maybe it's that I haven't yet built up enough knowledge and experience to recognize success when it happens.

### What Does Happiness — or Meaning — Even Mean?

At its core, I think happiness comes from the sense of continuous growth toward a goal, or from feeling that you're making a real contribution. The journey of working toward something meaningful and feeling it.

Happiness and meaning aren't perfectly identical, but they're close enough to treat as similar — like how θ ≈ sin θ for small angles. If there's enjoyment in that process of finding meaning, I've come to believe that a consistent stream of success experiences is absolutely essential.

### It Leads to Questions About How Teams and Organizations Operate

This is something that only works if team leaders and managers are on board, but I genuinely believe continuous success experiences are a critical ingredient in developing people (including leaders themselves). In time-and-materials businesses, keeping costs down by reducing the number of higher-billed leaders/managers and bringing in lower-cost external staff is directly tied to revenue. But if that's all you optimize for, you end up with rising attrition and chronically low motivation.

A slight tangent, but [Cybozu Inside Out - We're Publishing Our Development Team Creation Guide](https://blog.cybozu.io/entry/2023/07/26/170000) has a relevant quote worth sharing:

> Great teams generally achieve both task-related outcomes (customer satisfaction, on-time and on-budget delivery, high quality, etc.) and people-related outcomes (wanting to keep working on this team, feeling growth, feeling happy, etc.). The key is achieving both simultaneously.
>
> Overemphasizing task outcomes leads to burnout, low morale, and high turnover. Overemphasizing people outcomes produces a cozy but complacent team, or one that chases technical problems at the expense of the customer.

When you end up under a leader or manager who is excessively hands-off or does poor work (whatever their intent), I think that's what leads to people being thrown into some project they don't understand, in a state of confusion, with no sense of accumulated success.

### Programmer vs. Designer vs. Lead

Comparing programmers and designers to PLs — as a programmer or designer you can experience things like:

- Code I wrote (or directed an AI to write) working exactly as intended
- Implementing a valuable feature with maintainable code and solid tests
- Successfully proposing a directory structure that's lean and appropriate for the requirements
- Having the software we built used by a meaningful number of people and actually solving their problems

From design through to coding, there's something satisfying at each stage of the project. But when you're brought in as PL on a project that's beyond your current capability — or one that's already on fire — all of that is gone. That's the hard part.

As a project progresses, what you're doing (your decisions and measures) is based mostly on "this is probably right" — and you don't find out whether it actually was until the project is nearly done. That's part of why it's structurally hard to accumulate success experiences as a PL.

### So What Can You Do?

There are a few options available to the person in this situation. **None of them are a silver bullet, but they're at least tougher than a plastic bullet.**

1. Negotiate for your salary to reflect the difficulty of the PL role (very hard)
2. Ask your PM (senior staff) or sales team to assign you projects that match your current skill level (hard, with some luck involved)
3. Since continuous success is hard, shift perspective — ask your PM to divide responsibilities so you're not failing continuously (relatively easier)
4. **No matter what**, get a more experienced or knowledgeable employee assigned and share the PL workload (luck involved)
5. Or better yet (the best option): get assigned to a small, elite team that doesn't fall behind even if the PL does nothing — one that self-corrects even without the lead's intervention (luck involved, but the easiest)

There are probably other options, but those come to mind first.

The bottom line is: if something is unfulfilling, the only real move is to counter it with something equally or more fulfilling.

### Don't Forget That the PL Is Also a Team Member

I think the PL's role is to support the project's success — without delays, and without burning out the team. And the "without burning out" part absolutely includes yourself.

If being a PL isn't fulfilling, push for enough interesting work to compensate.

And fundamentally — **if a company or organization won't let you do interesting work, it's probably done for** — and changing companies or finding a new stage to operate on is also a valid move (fortunately, my company is a bit chaotic but does listen and respond when I raise things, so I'm grateful for that).

(And honestly — isn't that exactly why people who are good enough to be trusted with leadership roles don't stick around at places that won't let them do that?)

## Epilogue That's Barely a Summary

If only we got assigned to an incredibly capable dev team and project, I wouldn't need to think about any of this.

**All I want is to work on a team where everyone acts like a leader, where everyone manages the QCDS of their own work.**
