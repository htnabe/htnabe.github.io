## Things I Should Have Been More Careful About

If I list every small detail, it would never end, so I'll save those for another time (probably). For now, I'll focus on these four generally important points (in order of priority):

1. _Keep writing continuously without leaving long gaps_
2. _Stick to your initial plan as much as possible, and if you can't, update it continuously_
3. _Request regular and frequent reviews_
4. _Value your hobby/leisure time even more_

Below are some notes.

### Keep Writing Without Long Gaps

**If you can do this, you're basically 80% done with the paper.**

In my case, after my conference presentation, I ended up creating about **a 3-week period during spring break in March where I neither read nor wrote the paper**, and that delayed submission significantly.

After a break, it's easy to feel reluctant to start writing again, and it's especially painful when you have to revise the paper (at least it was for me).

### Stick to the Initial Plan, and Update It if Needed

I think this applies beyond paper writing too (it feels close to agile development).

In my case, not only did I leave long gaps, but I also found **unexpected data errors**, which forced a major plan revision.

I won't go into how to prevent `unexpected data errors` here, but I think the key is **how quickly you can correct course when accidental problems appear**. That matters not only for protecting your mental state, but also for writing a higher-quality paper.

### Request Reviews Regularly and Frequently

I eventually did this fairly well, but I wasn't doing it enough at the start, so I'm listing it here.

When I first started, I would revise every section (`Introduction` -> `Theory` -> `Numerical Example` -> `Conclusion`) and only then ask for a review. But if major corrections were needed early on, I had to delete or heavily rewrite most of the later parts.

That was very inefficient, so partway through I made two changes:

- _Revise sections in the order Theory -> Numerical Example -> Introduction -> Conclusion (main parts -> dependent parts)_
- _Request a review as soon as one section is revised_

People used to writing papers will definitely go "**well, obviously**," but this was something I couldn't notice early on.

Also, since I knew my reviewer was busy, at first I often thought things like "**Is it okay to ask about such tiny details (wording that doesn't affect content much)?**" But in the final phase, I asked without worrying too much.

**In the end, if I couldn't figure something out after a certain amount of time (45 minutes for me), I consulted the reviewer.**

### Value Hobby Time (Even More)

This one is different in tone from the others, but I list it because I think it's essential for keeping the writer mentally healthy.

As said in the linked slide deck "[How to Write Papers - Mainly Focusing on Mental Aspects](https://speakerdeck.com/kikuzo/lun-wen-falseshu-kifang-zhu-nimentarumian-falsedui-chu-wozhong-xin-tosite?slide=8)," paper writing is intense labor that can build stress even for full-fledged working adults. For students, it's probably even easier to get stressed.

I won't provide a mathematical proof, but **young == can overwork endlessly** is false.

Humans break when stress exceeds tolerance. _To write good papers_ (or _to do good work_), I believe it's extremely important to enrich the time you spend outside paper writing.

## Final Thoughts

Writing papers is hard, but being able to publish your own (not only your own, of course) results to the world is one of the most rewarding things for researchers and engineers.

I still don't know whether I'll end up in a workplace where I can keep writing papers (I'm job hunting hard right now), but regardless, this was a great opportunity to deeply understand both "**how tough research can be in many different ways (seriously too many)**" and "mindset for writing formal documents, etc."
